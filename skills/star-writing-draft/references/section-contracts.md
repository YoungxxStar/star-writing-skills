# Section contracts

Use these contracts as diagnostic defaults. Adapt, combine, rename, or omit sections when the paper's argument requires it.

## Title

- Name the scientific object, question, contribution, or capability.
- Preserve the actual scope.
- Avoid claims that require evidence the paper does not provide.

## Abstract

- Make the paper's question, contribution, evidence, and boundary recoverable without the main text.
- Select the evidence that best tests the central claim.
- Keep attribution and aggregation unambiguous.
- Avoid introducing technical detail that does not clarify the core idea.

## Introduction

- Establish the research setting and why the unresolved requirement matters.
- Locate the precise boundary of prior capability.
- Present the insight and contribution at the level needed to motivate the paper.
- Make the promised claims match the later evidence.
- Avoid turning the introduction into a literature inventory or method specification.

## Related work or background

- Give the reader the concepts and comparisons needed to locate the contribution.
- Organize sources by question, capability, assumption, or boundary.
- Distinguish the cited work's actual object, input, output, and evidence.
- Avoid miniature paper summaries and artificial deficiency claims.

## Problem statement or formal setup

- Define objects, assumptions, available information, target quantities, and scope.
- Separate the general problem from a particular dataset, apparatus, or implementation.
- Make symbols and terms sufficient for later reasoning.
- Avoid using the proposed solution to define the problem it claims to solve.

## Theory, mechanism, or method

- Explain why the design follows from the problem or hypothesis.
- Separate assumptions, derivations, mechanisms, interfaces, and implementation choices.
- Match equations and procedural claims to what is proved or implemented.
- Define every symbol, domain, index, unit, quantifier, and convention before it
  is needed; make every “denotes” statement map objects completely and
  unambiguously.
- Keep prose, equations, diagrams, algorithms, and executed implementation
  consistent about inputs, outputs, timing, and scope.
- State what is invariant and what is specific to the chosen instantiation.

## Data, materials, or study design

- Establish how observations were produced, selected, split, measured, and processed.
- State controls, sampling units, exclusions, and sources of variation.
- Distinguish exploration, development, selection, and final evaluation.
- Preserve enough information to interpret the evidence.

## Experiments or evaluation

- Organize comparisons by research question.
- Define the tested variable, baseline, metric, aggregation, uncertainty, and selection rule.
- Make each experiment answer a stated claim or alternative explanation.
- Include limiting cases needed to define the contribution's boundary.

## Results

- Answer the research question before narrating details.
- Report decisive quantitative, qualitative, theoretical, or mixed evidence.
- Distinguish observation from interpretation.
- Explain heterogeneity, exceptions, and uncertainty where they affect the conclusion.
- Avoid reading tables cell by cell.

## Discussion

- Interpret what the results change in the relevant field.
- Compare plausible explanations without presenting speculation as fact.
- State transfer conditions and practical or theoretical implications.
- Integrate limitations where they sharpen the conclusion.

## Limitations

- Identify the boundary created by assumptions, evidence, design, scale, population, domain, or evaluation.
- Explain how the limitation affects interpretation.
- Avoid generic disclaimers and hypothetical weaknesses unrelated to the claims.

## Conclusion

- State what question was answered and what the evidence established.
- Preserve the claim boundary.
- End with a specific implication or unresolved direction.
- Avoid new evidence, new claims, and generic future promises.

## Supplementary material or appendix

- Extend verification, interpretation, or reproducibility.
- Include detailed derivations, protocols, controls, exhaustive analyses, and additional examples when they support the main argument.
- Retain explicit links to the claims or methods they support.
- Avoid using the supplement as storage for material with no argumentative or reproducibility role.
