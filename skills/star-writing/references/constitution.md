# STAR Writing Constitution

A research paper is a bounded, auditable argument. It is not a project diary,
a catalogue of components, or a promotional document.

A scientific story is evidence-preserving compression: it removes cognitive
load without inventing a gap, mechanism, result, or implication.

## The three contracts

### Scientific contract

Fix the research object, available information, assumptions, intervention or
study design, output, comparison class, scope, and failure conditions. A method
name does not substitute for this contract.

### Evidential contract

For every important claim, identify the direct evidence, the reasoning that
connects evidence to claim, the uncertainty, the alternatives excluded, and
what the evidence cannot establish.

### Reader contract

At each point, give the reader the definitions and context needed to understand
why the next statement appears and how it advances the central argument.

When any contract changes, audit the other two. Most manuscript drift occurs
when the scientific object changes but the evidence or exposition does not.

## Stable principles

1. **Establish truth before constructing the story.** Determine the responsible
   source for each kind of claim.
2. **Define the object before naming the solution.** State the objective,
   formulation, available information, assumptions, output, and scope.
3. **Treat novelty as relational.** Compare against the closest alternative
   under the same problem and information contract.
4. **Challenge the strongest alternative explanation.** Ask whether an observed
   gain could come from more information, resources, tuning, selection, or a
   simpler intervention.
5. **Keep claim strength below identification strength.** More observations do
   not repair a design that cannot identify the conclusion.
6. **Separate observation, construct, inference, mechanism, and implication.**
   Do not present an interpretation or possible use as a direct observation.
7. **Separate novelty, validity, mechanism, and utility.** Each needs different
   evidence.
8. **Make scientific statements traceable.** Every number has a statistical
   contract, every citation supports a proposition, and every visual has an
   inferential role.
9. **Name the axes of generality.** Breadth on one axis does not imply breadth on
   another.
10. **Preserve uncertainty and negative evidence.** Boundaries and failures
    define where the claim holds.
11. **Build the argument before polishing prose.** Organization follows the
    reasoning, not a universal surface template.
12. **Compress without losing recoverability.** Preserve the object,
    comparison, evidence, aggregation, and qualifier needed to reconstruct the
    meaning.
13. **Propagate semantic changes globally.** A changed definition, term, result,
    or scope may affect the title, abstract, methods, results, captions,
    conclusion, and supplement.
14. **Treat revision as semantic version control.** Preserve current author
    intent, edit only the authorized scope, and record why material
    interpretations change.

## Claim-specific sources of truth

Do not impose one universal evidence hierarchy.

- Verify procedural and implementation claims against the executed protocol,
  instrument or apparatus record, code, configuration, and environment
  appropriate to the study.
- Verify empirical claims against the underlying observations or outputs,
  analysis or evaluation logic, and aggregation.
- Verify mathematical claims against definitions, assumptions, and derivation.
- Verify literature claims against the cited primary source at the supporting
  passage.
- Verify reproducibility claims against the files and instructions actually
  released.
- Verify submission constraints against current official venue material.
- Treat application value as prospective unless tested in the claimed use.

Manuscript prose records a claim; it does not validate that claim.

## The inferential ladder

Keep these levels explicit:

1. **Observation:** what the artifact or analysis directly shows.
2. **Construct:** what the measurement operationalizes and its limitations.
3. **Inference:** the bounded conclusion supported by the observation.
4. **Mechanism:** the explanation for the result, requiring separate
   identification.
5. **Implication:** potential scientific or practical value, requiring
   downstream evidence for a realized claim.

Use the strongest accurate statement supported at the identified level. Do not
weaken established results reflexively, and do not strengthen them through
adjectives or causal verbs.

## Compact decision tests

- Can each central claim be traced to direct evidence and an explicit warrant?
- Can each experiment or visual be traced back to a necessary claim?
- Does each citation support the exact clause where it appears?
- Is the independent unit, comparison, denominator, and aggregation of each
  empirical number recoverable?
- Would the conclusion survive the strongest plausible alternative
  explanation?
- Does the reader know each object before it is used?
- Can a local semantic change be found everywhere it propagates?

If any answer is no, fix the scientific or evidential contract before polishing.
